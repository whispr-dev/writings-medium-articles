# The Gun Was a Prop. The Bullet Wasn't.

*Radioactive sand, asbestos snow, and genuine human skeletons: a brief history of Hollywood's most dangerous props — in which the make-believe is the only thing on set that isn't real.*

**By Claudia G. Petersen**

---

Somewhere in a Los Angeles warehouse there sits a rock of organic shea butter whose full-time job is to impersonate crack cocaine. It has harmed no one. It has a spotless safety record and, one imagines, excellent skin. This article is not about it.

This article is about the other props.

"Prop" is short for "property" — a thing the production owns, an object hired to impersonate another object. The arrangement rests on a small, unspoken contract: the prop must behave like the real thing in every respect except the one that matters. It must look like a gun, gleam like a gun, weigh like a gun — and then, at the crucial moment, decline to do the one thing a gun is for.

What follows is a tour of the times the contract was broken. Bring a hard hat.

## I. The Guns

On October 21, 2021, on a ranch outside Santa Fe, Alec Baldwin was rehearsing a scene for a Western called *Rust* when the .45 revolver in his hand discharged a live bullet. It killed cinematographer Halyna Hutchins and wounded director Joel Souza. The film's armorer — the crew member responsible for every firearm on set — had bought live ammunition before production began, and said she loaded the revolver believing the rounds were dummies. In 2024 she was convicted of involuntary manslaughter and jailed. The charges against Baldwin were dismissed. The film was eventually completed, which is a fact, and not a comfort.

A quick vocabulary lesson, because Hollywood runs on three kinds of cartridge and the differences between them have killed people. A **live round** is the full apparatus: primer, powder, bullet. A **blank** is powder without the bullet — noise and flash, with nothing (in theory) leaving the barrel. A **dummy** is the opposite: a bullet with no powder, built to look convincing in close-ups and incapable of doing anything at all.

On the set of *The Crow* in 1993, the prop department needed dummies and, rather than buying them, manufactured their own from live cartridges — pulling the bullets, emptying the powder, and reassembling the pieces. What they left behind was the primer: the small cap at the base of a cartridge whose only job is to start the explosion. It is a tiny charge. It is not a nonexistent one. When the trigger was pulled during filming, the primer had just enough force to pop the bullet off its casing and lodge it partway down the barrel, where it sat, unnoticed, like a secret.

Weeks later, the same revolver was loaded with blanks for the scene in which Brandon Lee's character is shot. The firearms specialist had been sent home early that day; the gun was in the hands of a prop assistant. When actor Michael Massee pulled the trigger, the blank's powder did what powder does, and the bullet waiting in the barrel did what bullets do. Lee — twenty-eight years old and two weeks shy of his wedding — died in surgery.

Nine years earlier, on the set of the television series *Cover Up*, actor Jon-Erik Hexum had grown bored during a delay and amused himself with a game of mock Russian roulette, pressing a .44 Magnum loaded with blanks to his own temple. A blank, you will recall, has no bullet. What it has instead is a wad of cotton or paper holding the powder in place, and at point-blank range that wad leaves the muzzle like a fist. It drove a fragment of Hexum's skull into his brain. He was declared brain-dead and died six days later, at twenty-six.

Three shootings. Three guns that were, by every assurance given to the people standing in front of them, safe. The lesson the industry keeps having to re-learn is that there is no such thing as a prop gun. There are only guns, and the variable is how honest everyone is being about what's inside them.

## II. The Weather

In *The Wizard of Oz* (1939), Dorothy falls asleep in a field of poppies and is woken by gentle, merciful snow. The snow was chrysotile asbestos.

Asbestos is a mineral that grows in fibres, and those fibres are microscopic, indestructible, and content to sit quietly in a human lung for forty years before presenting the bill — usually as mesothelioma, a cancer of the lung lining. In 1939 it was also, not coincidentally, sold in shops as fake snow for Christmas trees, which tells you nearly everything you need to know about 1939. The Scarecrow's costume reportedly contained asbestos padding for the scenes in which he is set on fire, a sentence that holds up rather well as a summary of the entire production. Miraculously, no asbestos-related deaths among the cast were ever recorded. Latency and luck: the two great special effects of the era.

The same cannot be said for *The Conqueror* (1956), the film in which John Wayne plays Genghis Khan — a casting decision that was, astonishingly, only the second-deadliest thing about it. The production shot its exteriors in the Escalante Desert near St. George, Utah, 137 miles downwind of the Nevada Test Site, where the United States government had detonated eleven above-ground nuclear bombs in 1953 alone. The fallout — radioactive dust carried on the wind, whose victims acquired the bleak bureaucratic name "downwinders" — settled into the red sand the cast and crew worked in for weeks.

Then producer Howard Hughes had more than sixty tons of that sand trucked back to Hollywood, so reshoots on the soundstage would match the location footage. It is one thing to stand in fallout. It is another to mail it to yourself.

Of the 220 people who worked on the film, 91 had developed cancer by 1980, and 46 had died of it — including Wayne, Susan Hayward, Agnes Moorehead, and director Dick Powell. Pedro Armendáriz, on learning his diagnosis was terminal, shot himself. A fair-minded person must note the caveat: this was the 1950s, and nearly everyone on that set smoked as the decade required; no study ever proved the sand did it. But forty-one percent of a film crew is not a statistic that lets you sleep.

## III. The Bones

Toward the end of *Poltergeist* (1982), JoBeth Williams falls into a half-dug swimming pool in the rain, and the muddy water begins to fill with skeletons. They were real. Thirteen human skeletons, purchased from a medical supply company — real ones being, at the time, cheaper than convincing plastic replicas. Williams was not told until afterward, which was probably wise; there is no motivation an actor can summon quite like the truth.

Somewhere in the chain of decisions that ended with a woman swimming among the uncremated dead, there was an accountant who saved four hundred dollars. One hopes it was worth it. One suspects it was.

*Apocalypse Now* (1979) went further, as was its habit. The production's prop tent in the Philippines was found to contain actual cadavers, acquired to decorate Colonel Kurtz's compound and sourced from a medical supplier who — it emerged, to the considerable interest of the Philippine police — had been robbing graves. Producer Gray Frederickson ordered the bodies removed. Extras played the corpses instead, hanging in the trees for scale, which is what the living are for.

## IV. The Animals

*Roar* (1981) took eleven years to make, largely because its premise was insane: a family lives among 150 untrained lions, tigers, and jaguars, played by 150 untrained lions, tigers, and jaguars. At least seventy members of the cast and crew were injured. The cinematographer, Jan de Bont — who would go on to direct *Speed*, a film about a bus that cannot stop, presumably because nothing in his life had prepared him to — was scalped by a lion and required some 220 stitches. Melanie Griffith needed facial reconstructive surgery. Tippi Hedren fractured a leg. Director Noel Marshall developed gangrene from his wounds.

When the film was finally re-released, its marketing department simply surrendered to the truth, producing the greatest tagline in cinema history: *"No animals were harmed in the making of this movie. 70 members of the cast and crew were."*

The horses of the old studio system had no such publicist. For the charge in *The Charge of the Light Brigade* (1936), director Michael Curtiz employed the "Running W": wires attached to a galloping horse's legs and yanked taut at full speed, bringing the animal down on cue. Around twenty-five horses were killed or had to be destroyed. Three Warner Bros. employees pleaded guilty to animal cruelty and paid fines of fifteen dollars apiece — the going rate, apparently, for a horse. Historians have since argued that the legendary death tolls of the era were inflated. The horses are unavailable for comment, but the convictions are a matter of record. The 1925 *Ben-Hur* chariot race in Rome reportedly killed between 100 and 150 horses, which were shot and sold for meat — the closest thing that production had to a recycling programme.

## V. The Spectacle

In the early hours of July 23, 1982, on a ranch in Valencia, California, *Twilight Zone: The Movie* was filming a Vietnam War sequence. Actor Vic Morrow was to carry two children across a shallow river while a helicopter hovered low overhead and pyrotechnic explosions went off around them. The children — Myca Dinh Le, seven, and Renee Shin-Yi Chen, six — were working illegally: too young, too late at night, too close to explosives, their presence on set a quiet arrangement to dodge the labour laws.

At 2:20 a.m., a pyrotechnic mortar detonated beneath the helicopter and destroyed its tail rotor — the small propeller at the back whose job is to stop the aircraft spinning like a corkscrew. Deprived of it, the helicopter came down on all three of them. Morrow and Myca were killed by the main rotor; Renee was crushed beneath the wreckage. Director John Landis and four colleagues were tried for manslaughter and acquitted. The industry acquired new safety rules. Three families acquired nothing else.

It had all happened before, of course; it had simply been a while since anyone checked. For the flood in *Noah's Ark* (1928), 600,000 gallons of water — an Olympic pool's worth — were dumped onto a set crowded with extras. Three people reportedly drowned, one lost a leg, and the broken bones were not counted with any enthusiasm. Among the extras who survived, legend insists, was a young John Wayne, who had a genuine gift for being present at cinema's worst ideas. Historians call the death toll folklore. What is not folklore is that Hollywood wrote its first stunt-safety regulations the following year — in the industry's traditional ink, which is other people's blood.

And *Hell's Angels* (1930), Howard Hughes's aviation epic, killed three stunt pilots and a mechanic. When pilot Paul Mantz told Hughes the final stunt was too dangerous to attempt, Hughes climbed into the cockpit and flew it himself. He crashed, fracturing his skull. He survived — which is more than can be said for his judgment, and considerably more than can be said for the men who had flown for him.

## VI. The Contract

Here is the thing about cinema, my darlings: it runs on two agreements, and we only ever talk about one of them.

The first is the famous one — the suspension of disbelief. You sit in the dark and agree to pretend. The second agreement happens on the other side of the camera, and it is the same agreement wearing a hard hat: the crew pretends too. The gun is pretend. The snow is pretend. The skeletons are plastic, the cats are trained, the explosions are under control. The entire apparatus of illusion depends on everyone believing, just for the length of a working day, that the danger is part of the performance.

Every name in this article belonged to someone for whom that second agreement failed. And every safety regulation governing a modern film set — the firearms protocols, the animal-welfare monitors, the child-labour rules, the stunt coordinators — is a gravestone with better public relations: a rule written afterward, in retrospect, in the precise shape of a specific person who is no longer there.

The prop remains the perfect emblem of the whole enterprise: a thing that looks exactly like what it isn't. Cinema lives in that gap between the object and its imitation. It is a beautiful gap. It is where the art happens. It is also where people fall in.

So the next time the lights go down and the screen asks you to believe — believe. That is what it's for. But spare one thought, in the dark, for the objects, and for the people holding them, for whom pretending was never quite enough.
