I Sold a $150 Product and Made $18.68

That Was the Good Deal.

By Claudia G. Petersen
So how come I only get $18.68 from a product that sells for $150?
Here is where the money goes.

---

A customer walks into a shop and pays $150 for something I designed. The shop paid around $20 to have the boards fabricated, a few dollars more for the remaining parts, and roughly $5 in labour to assemble and pack it. Call it $25 landed on the shelf. Someone buys it. I get fifteen percent.
$18.68.
Before you decide I was fleeced: I negotiated that deal and it was a good one. Twelve and a half percent, plus two and a half for granting exclusivity. Fifteen is the top of the range in my industry. Most designers I knew were getting eight, ten, twelve.
And here is the part that should stop you, because it stopped me: that is the arrangement that saved the company.
Before it, we manufactured everything ourselves. We kept the entire margin - all $125 of it - and we were going broke.

---

The evening the arithmetic caught up with us
Six months in. Three of us, sitting on the floor around a coffee table in the front room, surrounded by circuit boards, resistor reels, anti-static bags and a printer churning out labels. We were sleeping on that room's sofas, because the bedroom was in use. More on that later.
The work was counting small things into small bags. Concentrating hard, because a single wrong component means a customer with a dead product and a support email you cannot afford to answer. Two hours per unit. It cramps your hands into claws and empties your head at the same time, which is a specific kind of awful - too dull to think, too fiddly to drift.
We had been at it for weeks. Fourteen-hour days, six and seven days a week.
And all three of us stopped at almost the same moment. Nobody announced it. We just ran out.

---

Somebody said the thing out loud: we're losing. Not "we aren't getting rich yet." Not "we're breaking even." We were selling units, the books showed a profit, and the three of us were personally sliding backwards every single month.
I remember the disbelief more than the despair. This could not be what it was like for everyone. Nobody builds a company this way. There had to be a mistake somewhere, and it had to be ours, because the alternative was that the whole model was a lie.
So we sat in it for a while. Then someone said: right. From scratch. Whole plan, whole budget, blank page.

---

The first thing we had been getting wrong
We had built the entire business on a number we had never questioned: what we paid ourselves.
We had treated our own wages as the flexible bit. The bit you squeeze when things are tight. Every bootstrapping story you have ever read encourages this - the founders eating instant noodles, sleeping under the desk, paying themselves last. It is presented as virtue.
It is an accounting error.
When you underpay yourself, you are not funding the business. You are lending it money at a rate you never agreed to, from an account with no overdraft, and you are hiding the real cost of the operation from yourself. The books said we were profitable because the books did not contain the price of the labour that made everything. We were the subsidy.
So we rebuilt the numbers with our wages fixed at the top instead of scraped from the bottom - and not survival money either. What we actually needed to live a life we would still want in year three. Rent somewhere decent. Good food. Time off.
Then we ran the model.
The numbers did not work. Not "tight." Not "needs another push." They did not work, and no amount of effort was going to make them work, because the constraint was not effort.
That was the most useful evening of my working life.

---

If your model only closes when you underpay yourself, you do not have a business. You have a job with extra paperwork and worse hours, and you own the downside.
What we actually liked
Once the wage was non-negotiable, the question changed from how do we work harder to which parts of this are worth doing at all.
Easy answer. We loved the concept stage. Coming up with ideas for cool things. Designing the circuit. Drawing the front panel, moving the knobs around, watching a scribble turn into an object you could hold. That part was joy.
We hated everything after it. Counting parts into bags. Soldering. Packing. Stock. Shipping.
So we stopped doing it. We stopped selling products and started selling designs. Someone else fabricates, assembles, stocks and sells. We take a royalty per unit.
Fifteen percent of $150 is less than a hundred percent of $150. It is also, as it turns out, considerably more than a hundred percent of a business that quietly consumes its founders.

---

The thing nobody tells you about buying parts
Here is the mechanism underneath all of it, and almost nobody outside manufacturing knows it exists.
Look up a component on a real industrial supplier - not a consumer site, factory sourcing places, the ones with no slick graphics, just monotype text and a pciture of something industrial loooking. You will not find a price. You will find a ladder:
- 1 unit: $1.50
- 10 units: $1.10
- 1,000 units: $0.75
- 10,000 units: $0.10
Same part. Same factory. Same day. The price depends entirely on how many you can pay for at once.
Once you understand this, it changes how you design. We started building circuits that reused one value of potentiometer in three positions instead of specifying three different values - not because it was better engineering, but because it collapsed three orders of 4,000 into one order of 12,000 and crossed a price break. Multiply that across 150 components and some of them fall by two thirds.
Read that again, because it is the strange part: our bank balance was shaping our schematics. The circuit was not purely an engineering document. It was partly a financial one.
Maybe that's the wrong way to frame it. Good engineering isn't just the perfect solution according to physics. or the best sounding one to musicians. It has to also be the one that works financally. The one that appeals to customers even if it means putting a knob in less than engineeering ideal position. The one that conforms to your rule of "inputs always on the left, outputs always on the right" even when it's awakward to design - consistency matters sometimes. My point is good engineering satisfies every aspect of the brief.
But crossing price breaks means buying 10,000 of something when you needed 4,000. Which means building 250 units when you had orders for 100. Which means capital, storage, stock management, and a shop willing to take 25 instead of 10.
Better though, once you are over that line, everything compounds. Cheaper parts, better margins, more cash, bigger orders, cheaper parts again. It genuinely does get easier, faster, better.
It is getting over the line that breaks people. When you need $10,000 of stock and you have $9,000, you do not have a shortfall of $1,000. You have two completely different companies, and you are standing in the wrong one.

---

The uncomfortable conclusion
Everything costs more when you are small.
Not as a metaphor about disadvantage. Literally, per unit, on the invoice, in a table published by the supplier. The small company pays more per part than the large one, which raises its costs, which limits what it can buy, which keeps it small.
Effort does not move you across a price break. Talent does not. A better product does not. Only cash does.
Every business book you have read is written by someone who cleared that gap, usually with money that came from somewhere they mention in one sentence and never return to, and who then reverse-engineered a story about discipline and vision to explain it. The gap is the actual story. It just does not make anybody feel inspired, so it gets left out.
That is the honest version, and I would rather you had it than the inspiring one.

---

The question to answer before day one
Not day one. Day minus one. Before you register anything.
What is this actually for?
Because "I want to be rich," "I want to make a living doing something I love," "I want to run this industry," and "I want to build something that outlasts me" are four different reasons, and they produce four incompatible companies. Pick the wrong one and you will spend a decade doing the right work in the wrong shape.
Do it properly. If the answer is "a job I love," ask yourself whether you still love it in year three, doing the same task for the four hundredth time, exhausted, with the novelty gone and your family asking where you are. That is not a rhetorical question. It is the actual job.
I chose small. Niche, boutique, deliberately unscalable. It means I will never be a player in this industry. It also means fifteen years later I am still here, still designing, and I have not lost a night's sleep to a payroll I could not meet. I'm still happy.

---

One last thing
The company is called RYO.
Everyone in the industry assumes it stands for Roll Your Own, because we make build-it-yourself kits, which is what we sell. It is a good guess. It is not where the name came from.
It came from the bedroom.
The one we could not sleep in, because it was full of plants, because we needed to find $10,000 for a parts order and we had $9,000, and the gap had to come from somewhere.
Fifteen years later, that name is still printed on every panel we ship.

---

Epilogue
You may have noticed the bio, bottom of page. It says CEO@RYOModular.
By the time you read this, it may not. I sold the company.
Not because it failed. Fifteen years, still profitable, still shipping, still respected. Because I want to do other things, and RYO deserves someone with more appetite for its next fifteen years than I have for mine.
There's one thing left to say, and it's the half of this article I owed you.
I spent the whole piece explaining that only capital crosses the gap. Not effort, not talent, not a better product. Cash. It's true, and it's the most demoralising thing I know about business, and I never told you where the cash is supposed to come from if you don't already have it.
Here it is. There is exactly one way to manufacture capital out of nothing but time: build something for long enough that it stops being a job and starts being an asset somebody wants to own.
And the strange part, the part nobody tells you, is that the asset was never the products.
It's the fifteen years of nothing going wrong.
No black marks. No customer left hanging. No tax disaster. No week where I said something stupid in public and watched a brand evaporate overnight. All the boring, obvious advice that every article like this one hands you and every reader skims past - be decent to people, pay an accountant, don't be a dick on the internet - none of which feels like building anything at the time. It feels like merely not screwing up.
That turns out to be the product. A reputation is invisible right up until the day somebody puts a number on it, and then it is the number.
So there's no dramatic ending here. No collapse, no triumph, no lesson about vision. I built something small on purpose, ran it carefully for a long time, and converted it into the one thing I never had on day one.
There's no shame in taking the cheque.
That was always what the cheque was for.
