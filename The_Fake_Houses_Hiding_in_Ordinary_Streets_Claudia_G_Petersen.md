# The Fake Houses Hiding in Ordinary Streets

*The doors are numbered. The windows are immaculate. The only thing missing is the house.*

**By Claudia G. Petersen**

*[OPENING PHOTOGRAPH — front view of 23–24 Leinster Gardens]*

*Take ten seconds. What, precisely, is wrong with this terrace?*

Before you read another word, look at the photograph above. Which detail has betrayed the place? Is one of the windows too flat? Are the balconies repeating themselves a little too obediently? Why do two front doors appear to have no appetite for visitors, letters or, for that matter, opening?

Nothing is ruined. Nothing leans. There is no villainous crack zigzagging through the stucco. The terrace is so composed that it seems to have been born mildly disappointed in the rest of London. And yet, somewhere in that row, ordinary architecture quietly stops being architecture at all.

Have you found it?

Now let us walk around the back.

## The houses with no behind

Numbers 23 and 24 Leinster Gardens, in Bayswater, have doors, windows, balconies, columns and the serene social confidence of extremely expensive property. What they do not have is an inside.

They are a screen wall. Behind their painted windows there are no curtains, no breakfast tables and no one wondering where the good scissors have gone. There is open air, railway track and the London Underground.

The deception dates to the construction of the Metropolitan District Railway in the late 1860s. Much of the early Underground was made by **cut and cover**: workers dug a trench from street level, laid the railway in it, built a brick roof over the top and restored the world above as best they could. It was less like secretly burrowing beneath London and more like performing major surgery while insisting the patient remain dressed.

At Leinster Gardens, [two terraced houses had to be demolished](https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/building-victorian-underground-railway/) to make way for the line. The uncovered section also allowed the railway’s steam locomotives to release heat, smoke and steam before returning to the tunnels. The usual version of the story says that the false houses were built as a steam vent. The more exact—and more revealing—version is that the railway required a gap, while respectable London required the gap not to look like one.

So the engineers replaced the missing homes with roughly five feet of scenery. The windows were painted onto masonry. The doors acquired neither letterboxes nor useful handles. From the pavement, the rhythm of the terrace continued as though two entire buildings had not just been politely excused.

Victorian engineering was very good at transforming cities. It was equally good at making the machinery apologise to the neighbours.

The loveliest final detail is that the disguise has now acquired the status of the thing it imitates. Historic England lists the adjoining terrace and the [“screen wall forming equivalent of Nos 23 and 24”](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1221942) at Grade II. Britain is legally protecting a fake house for its authentic historic importance. Frankly, one could hardly ask for a more British sentence.

## Paris keeps the face and removes the building

Leinster Gardens is not an isolated architectural prank. Once cities began threading railways, electricity and other indecorous necessities through desirable streets, a small international species appeared: infrastructure in evening dress.

At **145 rue La Fayette** in Paris, the deception is particularly refined. The façade presents five storeys of proper Haussmannian order near the Gare du Nord. Its shutters remain permanently closed, but in Paris this can pass for temperament. Behind them, however, is not an apartment building.

During work on the RER B in the 1980s, the building’s interior was [entirely hollowed out](https://askip.ratpgroup.com/question/jai-entendu-quil-y-avait-de-faux-batiments-dedies-a-la-ventilation-dans-paris-a-quoi-servent-ils-concretement-et-ou-se-trouvent-ils/) and its street façade retained. The space became a ventilation shaft. Seen from above, the illusion ends rather abruptly: the elegant address has no conventional roof and no domestic life, only the anatomy of the railway below.

Darlings, if a three-metre ventilation fan must occupy a Parisian street, naturally one gives it good stonework.

The joke should not obscure the engineering. RATP says its underground network relies on [more than 500 ventilation installations](https://www.ratp.fr/ventilateurs). In ordinary operation, some move air at around 60 cubic metres a second to control heat and keep underground spaces tolerable. During a fire, they can move as much as 90 cubic metres a second to draw smoke away from danger. Those false windows are not concealing nothing. They are concealing one of the reasons thousands of people can travel beneath the city without having to think about breathing.

Paris has other disguised ventilation shafts at addresses including rue d’Aboukir and rue du Faubourg-Saint-Denis, while still other false façades conceal electrical equipment. There are enough of them that spotting the clues becomes a rather elegant urban game: shutters that never change, windows too uniformly dark, a grand entrance with no keypad or handle. A building may wear the expression of a home while having the private life of a turbine.

## Brooklyn’s brownstone goes down, not in

Across the Atlantic, **58 Joralemon Street** fits neatly among the brownstones of Brooklyn Heights. It is not quite the flat theatrical scenery of Leinster Gardens, nor the preserved skin of rue La Fayette. It began as a real Greek Revival home, built in 1847, before being acquired and gutted for the subway in the early twentieth century.

By 1908 it had become a ventilation plant and emergency exit for the Joralemon Street Tunnel beneath the East River. Its windows are now an unwavering black. Behind them are machinery, stairs and access to the transit system below—an interior, certainly, but not one in which a property agent could plausibly describe the lower level as a “cosy family snug”.

The building was renovated in 1999, including work to help its exterior continue blending into the historic neighbourhood. According to information gathered from New York’s Landmarks Preservation Commission and Transit Museum, the old house was [gutted rather than simply demolished](https://www.smartcitiesdive.com/news/fake-buildings-shrouding-transit-infrastructure-are-hiding-in-plain-sight/543142/). That makes number 58 a particularly delicious paradox: an authentic nineteenth-century house performing as a fake house so that it may carry out an entirely genuine job.

## Toronto gives electricity a bungalow

Not every impostor home is connected to a railway. In Toronto, several were designed to hide electrical substations—and unlike London’s paper-thin fiction, some were complete buildings constructed in the proportions of suburban homes.

[Electricity demand in the city rose by 75 per cent between 1945 and 1955](https://www.torontohydro.com/about-us/our-history). New substations had to be placed where the growing network needed them, including residential districts. This produced an awkward civic truth: everyone wanted reliable power; remarkably few people wished to gaze upon the equipment providing it.

Toronto Hydro’s answer was architectural camouflage. In the 1940s, some substations were designed to look exactly like the modest bungalows appearing around them. [The City of Toronto’s archives](https://www.toronto.ca/explore-enjoy/history-art-culture/online-exhibits/web-exhibits/web-exhibits-local-government/turning-on-toronto-a-history-of-toronto-hydro/turning-on-toronto-hiding-in-plain-sight/) show examples at **24 Malcolm Road** and **386 Eglinton Avenue East**, the latter built to echo the small homes that then occupied the street. Those bungalows have since been replaced by tall apartment buildings, leaving the substation still faithfully dressed for a neighbourhood that has moved on without it.

There is something unexpectedly touching about that. The machinery has kept its costume long after the audience changed.

## What, exactly, is the lie for?

It is tempting to treat these places as curiosities: pin them to a map, admire the trick and proceed to brunch. Yet fake houses expose a serious bargain at the centre of urban life.

A city is not merely its homes, parks, cafés and rather optimistic estate-agent descriptions. It is also a machine for moving air, current, water, waste, trains, data and millions of people. That machine requires shafts, transformers, tunnels, pumps and emergency exits. We depend upon them completely, but we generally prefer not to see them.

Sometimes concealment is simple courtesy. A continuous terrace is more pleasant than an industrial void. Camouflage can preserve a historic streetscape, reduce opposition to essential works and place noisy or hazardous equipment behind a controlled boundary. Beauty and utility need not conduct their relationship in public like a divorcing couple.

But concealment also has a price. When infrastructure disappears, so does our awareness of it. Electricity seems to arrive from a socket; clean air appears in a tunnel; water and waste perform their opposing journeys without troubling the breakfast mimosas. The more successfully a city hides the systems that sustain it, the easier it is to imagine that comfort is effortless—and that maintenance, labour, land and money need not enter the conversation.

Perhaps that is why these false homes feel more uncanny than an obvious industrial building. They borrow the visual language of private life: a door means somebody may answer; a window suggests somebody may be looking back. We see those familiar signs and furnish the rooms ourselves. A few rectangles and a street number are enough for the mind to invent inhabitants.

We began with a photograph and the question, *What is wrong with this street?* Perhaps nothing is. The façades are useful, beautiful and, in London’s case, historically protected. They allow the necessary and the lovely to occupy the same address.

Or perhaps the unsettling thing is not the fake house at all. It is how little evidence we require before deciding that a surface tells us what lies behind it.

The next time you pass a silent house with dark windows, you may wonder who lives there. A better question might be: **what does?**

Because once a door can be true only in paint, how much of the world do we recognise because we understand it—and how much because it has learned to look familiar?
