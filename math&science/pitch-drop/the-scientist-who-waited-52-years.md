# The Scientist Who Waited 52 Years to See a Drop Fall

*One black bead, one glass funnel, and an experiment with an impeccable instinct for when nobody was looking.*

**By Claudia G. Petersen**

What do you do when the event you have waited eighteen years to witness is finally imminent—but the object in question has a rather expansive definition of *soon*? How many nights do you surrender to it? And when exhaustion eventually sends you home, what are the chances it will choose precisely then?

On a Friday afternoon in April 1979, Professor John Mainstone telephoned his wife to say that he would not be home that evening. Something was about to happen in the physics department at the University of Queensland, and he meant to be there when it did.

He watched all Friday night. On Saturday, he called home again. He would be staying another night.

By Sunday evening, sleep had acquired the force of a natural law. Mainstone finally left. When he returned on Monday morning, the event had happened without him.

The object of this vigil had moved only a few centimetres. It had taken almost nine years to do so. The final movement lasted an instant.

Mainstone had missed a drop of pitch.

This was not even the first time the experiment had defeated an observer. It would not be the last. In fact, Mainstone would care for it for 52 years without once seeing a drop fall.

## A liquid with no respect for a human lifespan

The story began with Thomas Parnell, the University of Queensland’s first professor of physics. In 1927, Parnell heated a quantity of pitch, poured it into a glass funnel with a sealed stem and left it to settle. Three years later, he cut the stem open and gravity began its work.

Very, very discreetly.

Pitch is the black, tar-derived material once used to waterproof boats. At room temperature it feels solid. Strike it with a hammer and it can shatter. Leave it alone in a funnel, however, and it flows. The contradiction is only apparent: “solid” and “liquid” are not always tidy permanent identities. A material’s behaviour depends partly on the force applied to it and the timescale over which one is prepared to look.

Water rearranges itself immediately. Honey considers the proposal. Pitch would like several years and the weekend to think it over.

This reluctance to flow is viscosity. A [1984 analysis of the Queensland experiment](https://news.uq.edu.au/2014-11-10-explainer-pitch-drop-experiment) put the pitch’s viscosity at about 2.3 × 10^8 pascal-seconds—roughly 250 billion times that of water. The university’s current public description uses the rounder figure of [100 billion times](https://smp.uq.edu.au/pitch-drop-experiment). Either way, this is a liquid with formidable boundaries.

The first drop took eight years to fall, landing in 1938. By the time Mainstone became the experiment’s second custodian in 1961, only three drops had completed the journey. Five more would descend during his tenure. He did not see any of them do it.

There is a temptation to picture him sitting beside the apparatus for half a century like a scientific monk. He did not. The pitch drop was one responsibility in a full academic life, and the apparatus could spend years looking as though it had entered into a binding agreement with the funnel. But waiting does not have to mean staring. It can mean keeping something safe, checking it, explaining it, and arranging your life around the possibility that today—quite absurdly—might be the day.

Mainstone did all of that.

In 2005, [Parnell and Mainstone received an Ig Nobel Prize](https://news.uq.edu.au/2005-10-07-improbable-award-long-term-experiment) for patiently conducting the experiment. A prize for patience seems apt. One for punctuality would have been provocative.

## The experiment with comic timing

The 1979 vigil was perhaps the most punishing near-miss. According to [an account published by *The Guardian*](https://www.theguardian.com/education/2022/apr/30/its-literally-slower-than-watching-australia-drift-north-the-laboratory-experiment-that-will-outlive-us-all), he watched through two nights, gave in on the third, and found the drop in the beaker on Monday.

Nine years later, in 1988, another drop was ready. This time Mainstone stepped away for about five minutes to get a drink. When he returned, it had fallen. One might admire the pitch’s patience if its timing were not so suspicious.

By 2000, technology appeared ready to remove human frailty from the arrangement. A webcam could watch without eating, sleeping or suddenly remembering that it had a home to go to. Mainstone was in London when the eighth drop came due. In Brisbane, [a thunderstorm disrupted the power supply](https://www.theguardian.com/education/2022/apr/30/its-literally-slower-than-watching-australia-drift-north-the-laboratory-experiment-that-will-outlive-us-all), cutting the camera feed. The [University of Queensland’s own account](https://news.uq.edu.au/2013-10-18-world-famous-experiment-opens-history-books) records the result with brutal economy: the camera failed, and the drop fell. Mainstone’s verdict was equally concise: “The Pitch Drop has a mind of its own.”

The official history needs no stolen camera or elaborate caper. Reality supplied a drinks break and a blackout, both far more plausible and therefore rather crueller.

There is another correction worth making. You may have read that no pitch drop has ever been captured on film. That is no longer true.

In July 2013, a separate experiment at Trinity College Dublin finally delivered the goods. Its pitch had been placed in a funnel in 1944 and largely ignored for decades. Once researchers noticed that a new drop was forming, they installed a webcam. On 11 July, the drop detached in full view; [*Nature* reported it as the first pitch drop caught on camera](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature.2013.13418).

The famous Queensland experiment, however, retained its record of perfect evasiveness. A pitch drop had been filmed—just not *this* pitch drop.

[Mainstone died in August 2013](https://news.uq.edu.au/2013-08-26-custodian-uq-pitch-drop-experiment-dies), aged 78, after suffering a stroke. He had become custodian in 1961 and had spent the intervening 52 years preserving, publicising and occasionally being outmanoeuvred by Parnell’s funnel. Parnell himself had also died without witnessing a fall. The experiment had now outlived both the man who began it and the man who made it famous.

Then came the ninth drop.

## How to film a drop that does not drop

By 2014, Queensland was taking no chances. A time-lapse camera had been running since 2012, two more cameras were added, and tens of thousands of people around the world had registered to watch online. Surely three cameras and a small international audience could defeat one globule of pitch.

The pitch found a loophole.

Instead of breaking free, the ninth drop descended until it touched the mound formed by the eighth. It made a glancing contact and continued to stretch from the funnel above. There was no sudden plunge, no unambiguous frame separating *before* from *after*—only a change from an extraordinarily slow descent to an even slower one.

[Researchers eventually inferred](https://news.uq.edu.au/2014-11-10-explainer-pitch-drop-experiment) that contact occurred on 12 April by measuring that change in speed: from about 0.25 millimetres per day to 0.1. The cameras had recorded the process, but the long-promised fall had not happened.

Twelve days later, current custodian Professor Andrew White tried to replace the beaker beneath the apparatus. The seal around the old glass cover had degraded, so when the cover was lifted, the entire setup briefly rose with it and then dropped back onto its base. The jolt severed the thread of pitch. Eighty-four years after Parnell opened the funnel, the ninth drop was finally detached—not by gravity alone, but with a small contribution from routine maintenance.

This is one reason the pitch drop is more scientifically interesting than its joke premise suggests. It does not tick at a dependable rate. Parnell designed it as a demonstration, not a rigorously controlled experiment, and it has lived in cupboards, display cases and air-conditioned buildings. Seasonal temperature changes alter the flow. Air conditioning slowed it; at another point, hot display lights accelerated it. Even the shrinking weight of pitch remaining in the funnel changes the pressure driving it down.

The apparatus therefore demonstrates two things at once. First, a substance that looks solid can flow if time is allowed to make the argument. Second, even an experiment famous for doing almost nothing is never truly doing *only* one thing. The building warms and cools. Equipment changes. The material ages. People intervene. The observer waits—and is changed too.

As of 2026, [only nine drops have descended](https://smp.uq.edu.au/pitch-drop-experiment) from the original Queensland funnel. The tenth is forming, a live camera remains trained upon it, and more than 35,000 registered watchers from around 160 countries have joined the vigil. Guinness recognises it as the [world’s longest-running laboratory experiment](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/longest-running-laboratory-experiment) and says there is enough pitch for roughly another century.

It seems almost guaranteed that the experiment will require more custodians. It may require custodians who have not yet been born.

## The result that Mainstone did get

It would be easy to call Mainstone’s wait unsatisfied. In the narrowest sense, it was. He wanted to witness a drop fall and never did. There is no elegant scientific reframing that gives him those missing seconds back.

But a life’s work is not always the same thing as the moment it appears to be waiting for.

Mainstone inherited an obscure teaching demonstration—a funnel, a dark lump and a result arriving perhaps once a decade—and made people care about it. He preserved its continuity. He helped explain what it revealed. He told its story with enough affection that the world began watching too. The fact that thousands of strangers can now feel suspense while looking at something moving more slowly than a continent is, in no small part, his result.

Science is usually narrated as a succession of arrivals: the discovery made, the proof completed, the particle found. The pitch drop belongs to the less cinematic part—the measurements maintained, specimens tended, records handed on and questions kept alive by people who may never receive the answer personally.

Most of us quietly expect our lives to have plots. We begin a thing; therefore, somewhere before the final page, it ought to resolve for us. The experiment in Brisbane offers no such courtesy. It says that nature is not late simply because it is running beyond our appointment, and that unfinished does not mean futile.

John Mainstone never saw his drop fall. Yet because he waited, the rest of us know where to look.

Perhaps that is the final question suspended beneath Parnell’s funnel: how many things worth beginning—or worth faithfully tending—would we abandon if we knew that the ending belonged to someone else?
