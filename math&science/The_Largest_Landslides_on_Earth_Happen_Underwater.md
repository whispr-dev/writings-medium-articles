# The Largest Landslides on Earth Happen Underwater

*At a river mouth in British Columbia, scientists finally watched a natural submarine landslide unfold. The sand went downhill. The landslide went up.*

By Claudia G. Petersen

Where does your voice go when you call someone across an ocean? Through a cable on the seabed, most likely. The great bulk of intercontinental data travels through fibres laid across the ocean floor. The arrangement works so well that you can spend years complaining about the Wi-Fi without giving a thought to the stability of a slope off the coast of Africa.

In January 2020, a torrent of sediment-laden water tore down the submarine canyon off the mouth of the Congo River and broke two telecommunications cables. Internet service slowed across a stretch of coastline from Nigeria to South Africa. Scientists could follow the flow with instruments down the canyon. Watching a piece of seabed fail at the source of such a current was a different proposition.

For that, it helped to go somewhere smaller. At the head of a fjord in British Columbia, researchers pointed sonar at the steep underwater edge of a river delta and waited. When the slope gave way, its face began to retreat uphill, feeding a current that ran in the opposite direction. The water above had a tide to attend to. Below it, the landscape spent the better part of an hour coming apart.

## The edge of the river

The Homathko River empties into Bute Inlet, a long fjord on Canada’s Pacific coast. Each summer, melting snow swells the river and sends fresh sediment into the inlet. Some of that material settles at the river mouth. The delta continues beneath the water, where it has a steep front and an underwater channel system to carry away whatever comes loose.

In 2022, a team led by Rebecca Englert of the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute and Matthieu Cartigny of Durham University spent eighteen days studying the delta. The sonar imaged the steep face roughly once a second. Sound pulses travelled through the water and bounced back from the seabed and from sediment in motion. The returning echoes let the researchers follow changes that would have been difficult to see through the cloudy water with a camera. Daily maps showed where material had gone missing. A sensor moored 700 metres downslope registered when the water filled with suspended sediment.

The team documented about thirty small landslides and captured three in detail as they happened. Their study, published in *Science* on 12 November 2026, reports the first direct monitoring of natural landslides unfolding entirely underwater. The distinction is worth making. Geologists have been finding the scars and deposits of these things for years. It is the interval between those two pieces of evidence that has proved so hard to see.

## How sand goes downhill while the slope goes up

Take a steep face of tightly packed, waterlogged sand. To move past their neighbours, the grains have to spread apart a little. This opens up extra space between them, and water takes time to seep into that space. In the meantime, the water pressure between the grains drops. The grains press against one another more firmly, temporarily making the sand harder to shift. The very act of starting to move can briefly make the slope better at staying put.

As water seeps in and the pressure recovers, sand peels away from the exposed face. That reveals another layer, which can then go through the same process. The geologists call it **breaching**. Think of a cliff retreating as pieces fall from it. The pieces go down. The cliff edge moves back. At Homathko, that retreat took place at roughly two to twenty-seven millimetres a second, with occasional abrupt collapses interrupting the slower movement. The three closely watched failures lasted between 38 and 100 minutes. The millimetres measure how fast the face retreated, a rather different matter from the speed of what it sent down the channel.

The sand that came loose mixed with water. That mixture was denser than the surrounding water, so gravity sent it along the bottom as a **turbidity current**. The name comes from the cloudiness that suspended particles give the water. From above, something of this kind might look like a bit of murk. At the bed, it is a current with enough force to move sediment and, in some places, damage whatever people have laid across its path.

At Homathko, the currents travelled at least two kilometres. The water downslope stayed cloudy for twelve to fourteen hours. Across the observed slides, about 61,000 cubic metres of sediment disappeared from the delta front, roughly a fifth of the volume of sand the river had delivered over the same period. The failures coincided with high river flow and falling tides. That gives the researchers a promising clue about when to look for the next one. Establishing what starts each failure will take more observations.

## The size of the thing

Underwater landslides can be immense. About 8,150 years ago, the Storegga Slide off Norway displaced enough water to send a tsunami against the coasts of northern Europe. It is possible to put Storegga and Homathko in the same paragraph only because the word *landslide* is exceptionally accommodating. The Canadian events were small failures at a sandy delta. Their value lies in the detail with which they were observed. A giant slide on a continental margin may involve quite different sediment, forces and timing. Whether it generates a tsunami depends in part on how quickly and how much water it displaces.

Even a modest slide has consequences beyond the place where it started. A cable can be struck by sediment moving down a channel well away from the original failure. A pipeline or a wind turbine foundation can face a flow that keeps coming as the slope continues to shed material. The more reliably engineers can tell how long that flow might last, the better they can judge what a structure will have to endure.

The same currents also carry organic carbon that rivers deliver from land. Where that carbon ends up, and how much stays buried instead of being broken down, matters to our account of the ocean’s carbon cycle. A delta can look like the place where a river puts things down. Watch it for long enough and you can see how much of what it receives is on its way somewhere else.

## What the scar leaves out

Suppose you had mapped the Homathko Delta in the morning and returned the following day. You could find the missing sediment and measure the shape of the scar. Somewhere down the channel, you might find signs of the current. How would you tell whether the slope had gone in one great slip or fed that current in instalments?

There are ways to make an educated reconstruction from the shape of a scar or the layers in a deposit. The Homathko record gives those interpretations something to answer to. It even shows that a gradual retreat and a sudden collapse can occur in the same event. On a map made the next day, there is one scar where both things happened.

A broken cable comes with a timestamp. I wonder how often we mistake that sort of precision for knowledge of when a disaster began. Somewhere up the slope, the ground may already have been moving for quite some time.
