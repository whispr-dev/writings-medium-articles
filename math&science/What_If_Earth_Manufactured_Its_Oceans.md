# What If Earth Didn’t Receive Its Oceans—But Manufactured Them?

*Dry rock, hydrogen and an extravagance of heat may have given the young Earth a way to make water while it was still making itself.*

**By Claudia G. Petersen**

Who brought the water? It’s a reasonable question to ask of a planet that assembled itself in the warmer part of the young Solar System and now has a Pacific.

The Pacific is an especially awkward thing to explain away. You can stand at the edge of it for quite some time without feeling that someone has overestimated the amount. Yet the region where Earth grew was warm enough that water ice was harder to come by than farther out. Where, then, did we get an ocean you can spend half a day flying across? And who authorised the other ones?

The familiar answer is a delivery. Icy comets and water-bearing asteroids could bring water from colder regions. Some asteroids carry it in minerals, an arrangement that gives a stone the outward composure of something with absolutely nothing to declare. There is also evidence that rocky material resembling Earth’s original building blocks carried more hydrogen than researchers once thought. That would give Earth some of the ingredients for water from the beginning. All these sources could have contributed. The awkward part is working out how much each supplied. [Research on water-bearing asteroids](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-018-0413-4) · [Study of hydrogen in Earth-like meteorites](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32855337/)

In a paper published in *Nature Geoscience* on 24 September, Susmita Garai and colleagues at the University of New Mexico and the Lunar and Planetary Institute tested a different possibility. Suppose some of the rocks arrived dry. Suppose the atmosphere contained plenty of hydrogen. Could the rocks make water on the way down? [*Nature Geoscience* paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-026-02118-7)

## A very peculiar rain

Picture the young Sun surrounded by a disc of gas and dust. Some of that dust gathers into grains, and some of the grains build into pieces of rock millimetres to centimetres across. In one model of planet formation, an already growing body sweeps up large quantities of these pieces. Gas slows the pebbles as they pass, helping the planet’s gravity capture them. This is **pebble accretion**, which sounds like something you’d be asked to clear from a gutter and can, under the right circumstances, build a planet.

Growing Earth may have gathered a temporary atmosphere from the surrounding disc. That gas was rich in hydrogen. Heat from planetary growth could make the deeper parts of the atmosphere hot enough to melt incoming rocky material. To a pebble, the atmosphere would have been quite a long way from empty space. It would have been somewhere to spend enough time, at a high enough temperature, to come out chemically different. [*Nature Geoscience* research briefing](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-026-02138-3)

Garai and her colleagues recreated that encounter on a scale more agreeable to the university. They heated dry, iron-bearing rock samples to between 1,200 and 1,400 °C, passing hydrogen over some and helium over others. The runs lasted from 15 minutes to three hours. Helium let the team put the rock through comparable heat without giving it a gas that would readily take part in the chemistry.

In the hydrogen runs, metallic iron formed and the reaction produced water vapour. Under the more effective conditions, nearly four-fifths of the iron oxide was converted. The helium runs showed no comparable reaction. The rocks could be exceedingly hot in either gas. Only one gas had something to offer the oxygen. [University of New Mexico account](https://news.unm.edu/news/unm-research-suggests-earth-may-have-formed-much-of-its-water-as-the-planet-grew) · [Experimental data](https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.fttdz0980)

## What the rock gave up

Where was the oxygen? In the rock, chemically bound to iron. Hydrogen gas supplies molecules made of two hydrogen atoms. When one of those molecules takes up an oxygen atom from iron oxide, the result is H₂O. The iron left behind becomes metal. In this reaction, chemists call that change **reduction**. If you remember nothing else about the word, remember where the oxygen went. It left in the steam.

I like this detail because “dry rock” sounds so final. A dry stone can contain a great deal of oxygen. The oxygen is simply occupied. Give it hydrogen and enough heat, and some of it can leave in a form that will one day make a perfectly ordinary nuisance of itself at a picnic.

The metal matters too. The team found that the newly formed iron took up phosphorus from the surrounding molten rock. On a growing planet, dense metal can sink towards the centre, carrying some of that phosphorus with it. That sorting of a planet into a metal-rich core and a rocky mantle is called **differentiation**. The authors suggest that the process could help explain why Earth’s mantle has less phosphorus than Mars’s. A reaction overhead could leave evidence in the deep interior, long after the atmosphere that hosted it had gone. [*Nature Geoscience* paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-026-02118-7)

## A few hundred oceans, on paper

How do you get from a hot sample in a lab to an Atlantic? You need an estimate of how much suitable rock passed through the atmosphere, how much iron oxide it carried, and how much of it had time to react. Change the rock composition or the conditions and the total moves considerably. Under favourable assumptions, the researchers’ calculations reach hundreds of times the amount of water in today’s oceans. An “ocean” in that calculation is a unit of mass equal to all the water in Earth’s present oceans taken together. It’s a large enough unit that one might have expected to get by with it in the singular. [*Nature Geoscience* paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-026-02118-7) · [University of New Mexico account](https://news.unm.edu/news/unm-research-suggests-earth-may-have-formed-much-of-its-water-as-the-planet-grew)

The experiment established that the reaction can make water quickly. How much it made on Earth depends on how the planet grew, how much hydrogen it held on to at the time, and how much of the incoming material passed through the right conditions. Then comes the question of what happened to the water. It began as vapour. Some could have escaped into space. Some could have ended up deep in the planet. What remained at the surface needed a world cool enough for liquid water to persist.

A planet can have more than one source of water and more than one opportunity to lose it. A water-bearing asteroid could still have arrived after the reaction had run its course. The Pacific is free to have several origins. It’s under no obligation to make the bookkeeping pleasant.

## What would still have to go right?

Suppose a young rocky planet around another star gathers iron-bearing pebbles under a hot blanket of hydrogen. There is no obvious reason the oxygen in its rocks should observe different rules. If those conditions recur, some worlds may make water as a routine part of growing. A fortunate delivery of ice would be one way to get the job done among several. [*Nature Geoscience* research briefing](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-026-02138-3)

Would that make habitable worlds common? It might make one part of becoming habitable easier. You still need a planet that can keep water liquid somewhere for long enough for it to matter. Then there’s the matter of life beginning at all. We have a sample of one there, and we’re conducting the survey from inside it. It is a difficult position from which to judge how lucky we’ve been.

The next time I look out over an ocean, I’ll try to picture it before there was anywhere for a wave to break. Just rock falling through hot gas, oxygen leaving iron, and water beginning as something the young Earth could make. The shore, like everything that would eventually stand on it and ask where the water came from, would have to wait.
