# The “Magic Mushroom” With a Side of Death-Cap Chemistry

*On a rotting palm in Florida, scientists found a fungus that makes both psilocybin and amatoxins. Its genes suggest it came by the combination in two very different ways.*

**By Claudia G. Petersen**

What sort of mushroom are we talking about? The sort people seek out for a psychedelic experience, or the sort they’re warned about in the same tone of voice used for live electrical wiring?

We have found this a convenient distinction. The mushrooms, who were never consulted, appear to have exercised their right of reply.

On a moss-covered palm trunk in a Florida wetland, fungal researcher Andrew Kunik found a little brown mushroom called *Galerina indica*. In a study published on 11 November in *Current Biology*, Kunik and colleagues reported that the fungus produces psilocybin and psilocin, the principal psychedelic compounds in so-called magic mushrooms. It also produces two compounds from the amatoxin family, whose more notorious members can cause fatal liver failure. It’s the first mushroom species known to make both kinds of chemical.

One of the more unnerving things about it is how little the mushroom has done with this information, visually. It looks like a small brown mushroom. The most consequential part of the discovery is the part you’d need a laboratory to see. [University of Florida account](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/news/2026/11/11/uf-students-discover-the-dark-magic-mushroom/)

## Two chemistries, one mushroom

Kunik was documenting fungi at the University of Florida’s Ordway-Swisher Biological Station. Some members of the genus *Galerina* already had a reputation for making amatoxins. During handling, the Florida specimens also stained blue, a change that can occur in some psilocybin-producing mushrooms. This was a clue worth pursuing in a laboratory. The eye had more or less exhausted its usefulness.

The researchers used **mass spectrometry**, which sorts electrically charged molecules by how much mass they have for their charge. The team also broke the charged molecules into fragments and examined the resulting pattern. Think of taking apart a watch whose maker you don’t know. The size of the watch tells you something. The shapes of the pieces, and which ones fit together, tell you considerably more.

The samples yielded signatures for psilocybin, psilocin and two amatoxins. One of the two toxins, which the researchers named alpha-proamanitin, closely resembles alpha-amanitin, a major toxin in death caps. The familiar alpha-amanitin itself was absent from the samples. Sequencing the fungus’s DNA also turned up genes associated with making both kinds of compound. The chemistry showed what was present in the tissue. The genome pointed to how the fungus could make it. [Research paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2026.10.041) · [Full preprint](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13419790/)

Why does a slight difference between two toxin molecules matter? Because a toxin has to interact with something in the body, and even a small change can affect how well it does that. In death-cap poisoning, amatoxins interfere with an enzyme that copies instructions from DNA into RNA. Cells use those copies to make proteins. Disrupt the copying badly enough and cells can die, with the liver taking much of the damage. The team suspects that the changes in *G. indica*’s amatoxins may make them less potent. The study leaves the effects of eating this species unmeasured. There’s a great deal of daylight between identifying a relative of a notorious poison and knowing exactly what it will do. [CDC report on death-cap poisoning](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/66/wr/mm6621a1.htm) · [Full preprint](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13419790/)

## An inheritance from the side

The discovery raises a question that’s harder to answer with a test tube. Which chemistry came first?

The researchers compared the family tree of the fungi with trees drawn from the genes involved in making the compounds. A species tree traces how groups split from common ancestors. A gene tree does much the same job for a particular stretch of DNA. If a gene has passed down from parent to offspring throughout its history, you’d generally expect the two accounts to have something in common.

Here, the best explanation the authors found was that the ancestors of *G. indica* could already make amatoxins when a group of genes for psilocybin production arrived from another fungal lineage. DNA acquired across lineages in this way is called **horizontal gene transfer**. Once the newcomer DNA is in a genome, ordinary inheritance can carry it down through later generations.

A cluster matters because making a compound can take several steps. One gene can encode an enzyme that changes a starting molecule. Another can encode the enzyme that works on the result. Genes that sit together in the genome can travel together, giving the recipient several of the steps at once. Imagine inheriting a single brass cog. Now imagine inheriting the clock.

There may have been two such acquisitions within *Galerina*. Another specimen, from Mississippi, contained psilocybin and psilocin with no detectable amatoxins. The team’s comparison suggests its psilocybin genes arrived independently of those in *G. indica*. Nobody watched either transfer occur. The evidence is in the surviving DNA, where the genes and the species appear to have kept different family albums. [Research paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2026.10.041) · [Full preprint](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13419790/)

## Who is all this for?

Why would a fungus living on a rotting palm make a compound capable of changing human perception? Start with the things that actually encounter it. A little mushroom in a wetland is more likely to be investigated by a small animal looking for lunch than by anyone with an interest in the nature of consciousness.

The researchers raise the possibility that the two kinds of compound serve different roles in defence. A substance that discourages one animal may do very little to another. Even the word *discourages* covers several possibilities. An animal might eat less, leave sooner or grow more slowly after feeding. Each possibility would leave a different trail of evidence, and the study doesn’t establish which, if any, applies here. A human reaction to a molecule is a fascinating piece of chemistry and a rather parochial account of what the molecule is for. [Full preprint](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13419790/) · [Nature research highlight](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03639-2)

There’s also the intriguing possibility that the older chemistry changed around the time the newer chemistry arrived. The authors suggest that acquiring the ability to make psilocybin may have coincided with a reduction in amatoxin potency. The coincidence is itself an inference. We can see the two systems together in the living fungus. Working out whether one changed because of the other would take a different kind of evidence. It’s the sort of distinction a mushroom can afford to leave unresolved for several million years. We tend to have rather less patience.

## Whose invention is it, anyway?

A family tree can tell you who your great-grandmother was. It will have considerably less to say about where she got her best recipe. That question may lead out of the family altogether, even if everyone who cooks the dish today learned it from her.

A species tree can be perfectly sound and still leave room for the same sort of question. Follow the fungus back through its ancestors and you get one history. Follow a cluster of genes and, at some point, you may find yourself tracing a different line. Both histories belong to the mushroom on the palm. One explains its descent. The other helps explain what it can do.

So how much of an invention can a species call its own? *Galerina indica* makes these compounds in its own tissues. That much is settled. The making of the ability itself may have involved organisms that had long since parted company. What looks like a characteristic of one species, observed on one damp bit of wood, may be the surviving work of several.

We’d filed one of its chemicals under revelation and the others under poison. The mushroom had room for both. I wonder how many other distinctions nature has been quietly managing without.
