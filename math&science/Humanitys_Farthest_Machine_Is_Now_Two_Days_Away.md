# Humanity’s Farthest Machine Is Now Two Days Away—Even When Everything Works Perfectly

*Voyager 1 is a light-day from Earth. The extraordinary distance is also a rather unforgiving engineering problem.*

**By Claudia G. Petersen**

How long would you wait for a machine to answer?

A second? A minute? Long enough to begin pressing the same button with increasing moral conviction?

For the people operating Voyager 1, a straightforward exchange now takes approximately two days. Send a command on Monday morning. Even if the spacecraft responds immediately, its answer will not arrive until Wednesday.

Nothing has gone wrong in this example. This is the service working beautifully.

On 18 November 2026, Voyager 1 became the first human-made object to reach a distance of one light-day from Earth: about 25.9 billion kilometres. NASA confirmed the milestone after receiving its signal the following day. [1]

A light-day is a distance, despite sounding like something you might put in a diary. It is how far light travels in 24 hours. Radio waves travel at the same speed, which means a command needs roughly a day to reach Voyager and a reply needs another day to return. [2]

Those 48 hours are the travel time for one exchange. They do not include devising the command, checking it, processing the response or deciding what to do next.

The universe has supplied the minimum delivery estimate. Engineering must fit around it.

Voyager itself is travelling at roughly 61,000 kilometres per hour. That is extraordinarily fast by almost any useful terrestrial standard, and remarkably slow by the standards of light. The spacecraft needed nearly 50 years to reach a distance light crosses in a day. [2]

There is something quietly destabilising about that comparison. We have built a machine capable of surviving almost half a century beyond Earth, and space can still make the achievement look local.

The milestone also arrives with an uncomfortable detail.

According to NASA’s November updates, Voyager 1 stopped returning readable science and engineering data on 14 November. Ground antennas were still receiving its carrier signal: the radio transmission that normally carries the information. The signal and the spacecraft’s known trajectory allowed engineers to confirm its distance despite the missing data. [3]

It had reached somewhere unprecedented. It was having difficulty submitting the report.

The carrier is not meaningless. NASA says its presence shows that the spacecraft is maintaining its pointing towards Earth and that its antenna and transmitting equipment are working. The missing information suggests a problem in the system that collects and formats data for transmission; the cause remained under investigation in the updates checked for this article. [3]

That distinction matters. Hearing a transmission is different from receiving a usable account of the machine’s condition.

Imagine a remote building whose telephone keeps ringing your office but never delivers a voice. You know that something is powered. You do not yet know what happened inside.

And in Voyager’s case, your next question spends a day getting there.

The spacecraft carrying this burden left Earth on 5 September 1977. Its original work involved close encounters with Jupiter and Saturn. Its twin, Voyager 2, launched first but followed a different itinerary, eventually visiting Uranus and Neptune as well. [4]

Voyager 1 helped reveal active volcanoes on Jupiter’s moon Io and a nitrogen-rich atmosphere around Saturn’s moon Titan. These were discoveries about actual worlds, complicating any comfortable assumption that the outer Solar System consisted mainly of cold objects waiting to be photographed. [5]

It is easy, now, to treat Voyager as an endurance story with some science attached. The science was the point. Endurance has allowed the questions to change.

In 2012, Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause, the boundary of the heliosphere: the vast region inflated by the Sun’s outward flow of charged particles and associated magnetic field. Beyond it, the surrounding material is interstellar rather than dominated by the solar wind. [6]

This does not mean the spacecraft has passed beyond everything belonging to the Solar System. The distant Oort Cloud, a predicted reservoir of icy bodies orbiting the Sun, extends far beyond the heliosphere. A boundary in the particle environment is different from the outer extent of the Sun’s family of orbiting objects. [6]

Nor does “interstellar” mean Voyager is approaching another star. One light-day is only about one three-hundred-and-sixty-fifth of a light-year. The nearest neighbouring star system is more than four light-years away. [2]

Space permits you to leave one neighbourhood while remaining nowhere near the next.

Out there, Voyager’s measurements have offered something telescopes cannot provide in quite the same way: a sample of conditions taken by an instrument physically present in the interstellar environment. Its remaining magnetometer and plasma-wave instrument are listed as powered on, although the current data problem interrupts the return of their reports. [7]

There is a difference between being somewhere and being able to tell anyone what it is like.

The engineers have already demonstrated how much can be recovered across such a gap.

In 2024, they traced an earlier breakdown in readable data to a failed memory chip in Voyager 1’s flight data subsystem, the computer responsible for preparing information for transmission. Some of its software had occupied the damaged memory. [8]

Replacing the chip was unavailable as a maintenance option.

Instead, the team moved the affected instructions into functioning memory. No single free area was large enough, so they divided the code among several locations and adjusted the references that allowed the pieces to work together. A command sent on 18 April produced confirmation on 20 April that engineering reports were readable again. [8]

Think of a book with damaged pages. Saving the text is not enough if every reference still sends the reader to the missing section. The repair must preserve the directions as well as the words.

This was software maintenance performed on an object nobody could touch, with confirmation arriving roughly two days after the instruction left Earth.

It also explains why patience here is a technical discipline.

On a nearby machine, you can change something, inspect the result and revise your approach within seconds. At Voyager’s distance, each dependent step consumes another long exchange. A mistaken assumption can cost days; an unsafe command could remove the means of finding out what happened.

The relevant question becomes less “What should we try?” and more “What will this test tell us, and what must remain working afterwards?”

Meanwhile, the electricity is declining.

Voyager’s radioisotope thermoelectric generators turn heat from decaying plutonium into electrical power. They are not solar panels, and the fading supply cannot be improved by turning towards the Sun. NASA says each Voyager loses about four watts of available power a year. [9]

Four watts sounds almost offensively modest until the entire mission depends on finding them.

In April 2026, engineers shut down Voyager 1’s low-energy charged-particle instrument to conserve power, following an order of shutdowns agreed by the science and engineering teams years earlier. In September, they completed another power-saving change, replacing three powered devices with lower-power alternatives. [9, 10]

Keeping the mission alive therefore involves deciding which abilities it can afford to lose.

Longevity is not simply the same machine continuing unchanged. It is a succession of carefully managed reductions, each intended to preserve something worth doing.

Voyager is not helpless during the intervals when Earth cannot intervene. Its computers were designed with routines to detect faults and take corrective action, alongside the systems that keep its antenna pointed home. [11]

But distance does not automatically make a machine more capable. It makes dependence on immediate human help less workable.

That is the design problem Voyager places in front of its successors. What should a machine be allowed to decide alone? Which failures must it survive without consultation? How can it explain its condition when the people receiving the explanation have never seen its hardware?

And how do we preserve the knowledge required to understand it across changes of staff, institutions and generations?

A long-lived spacecraft is an archaeological object that may still require technical support. Its surviving instructions contain decisions made by people working with different tools, different assumptions and a different idea of the future.

One day, Voyager will no longer answer. Perhaps a component will fail beyond recovery. Perhaps the remaining power will become insufficient. Its departure from human conversation need not coincide with any dramatic change in its journey.

The object will continue. The relationship will have ended.

For now, people on Earth are still preparing questions for a machine a day away, then listening for an answer another day later. Their work preserves a connection between a place we cannot visit and an intention formed in 1977.

Voyager’s greatest measurement may eventually be not of interstellar space, but of how long human intention can remain legible inside a machine.
