# Some Stars Explode and Stay. Others Just Disappear.

*One star has survived at least 27 outbursts. Other stars may collapse into black holes without ever giving us the fireworks.*

By Claudia G. Petersen

How many times should a star be allowed to explode before we stop taking it personally? Would twice be enough? Five times? At twenty-seven, do you still rush to the telescope, or do you put the next one in your diary?

Astronomers did both. The star in question, AT 2016blu, lives in a spiral galaxy about 30 million light-years away. Between 2012 and June 2026, observers caught at least 27 outbursts. There was a loose rhythm to them, about 113 days, which is a rather demanding schedule for a star that appears to be having a final crisis. In March 2026, when it brightened again, astronomers turned NASA’s Chandra X-ray Observatory towards it. This time they could see what the visible light had been keeping from them. [Chandra’s observing account](https://chandra.cfa.harvard.edu/blog/node/979)

The day after NASA announced the result, a paper in *Physical Review D* made the case for a very different kind of stellar ending. Some massive stars may collapse without a bright supernova. The clue was a peculiar distribution of black-hole masses measured long after the stars that made them had gone. One study caught a star in the act of flaring. The other had to work backwards from what was left. [NASA’s announcement](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/chandra/nasas-chandra-finds-source-of-stars-mysterious-outbursts/) · [*Physical Review D*](https://doi.org/10.1103/mnzq-w1b5)

## The star that keeps turning up

AT 2016blu is a luminous blue variable, a hot, unstable giant estimated to have at least 33 times the Sun’s mass. Stars of this kind can shed gas and brighten dramatically while the star itself survives. Astronomers call some of these eruptions *supernova impostors*, a name that tells you something about how convincing the first impression can be. A genuine core-collapse supernova marks the end of the star as a star, even if it leaves behind a neutron star or a black hole. An impostor can show up again next season, with much the same grievances. [Aghakhanloo and colleagues’ study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20469) · [Chandra’s observing account](https://chandra.cfa.harvard.edu/blog/node/979)

The timing was the part worth getting out of bed for. An unstable star can flare for all sorts of reasons. When it keeps flaring at roughly the same interval, you start to wonder what else is keeping time. An orbit would do nicely. Put a companion on an elongated orbit and the two spend much of their time apart, then swing close at a predictable point. The encounters can recur on schedule even when the resulting outbursts vary.

Chandra had looked at this patch of sky in 2001 and 2002, before the first documented eruption. Those images yielded no detectable X-rays from AT 2016blu. During the March 2026 outburst, Chandra measured X-ray emission at least a hundred times brighter than the upper limit from those earlier images. The signal points to a compact companion, most likely a neutron star or a stellar-mass black hole. Which of the two it is remains an open question. [Chandra’s release](https://chandra.cfa.harvard.edu/press/26_releases/press_110926.html) · [Aghakhanloo and colleagues’ study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20469)

Here is the proposed arrangement. On each close pass, some of the giant’s gas falls towards the companion. It heats up and gives off X-rays. Those X-rays heat the surrounding gas, which glows in visible light. To a telescope looking only at visible wavelengths, the giant appears to be having another fit. Add the X-rays and you have evidence that the fit belongs to the pair. [Chandra’s account of the proposed mechanism](https://chandra.cfa.harvard.edu/blog/node/979)

There is a pleasing oddity here. One of the possible culprits is itself the remnant of a dead star. If the companion is a black hole, the recurring spectacle comes from gas heating up on its way towards an object whose own birth might have been considerably less conspicuous. Its birth is anybody’s guess. For a candidate we can actually look at, we can go much closer to home.

## The star that stopped turning up

In Andromeda, a massive star known as M31-2014-DS1 brightened in infrared light in 2014. By 2023, it was more than 10,000 times fainter in visible light. The astronomers who pieced together those observations think its core collapsed into a black hole, with the outermost material leaving a shroud of gas and dust. There was something to see, particularly in the infrared. What went missing was the brilliant supernova you might expect to find between the pictures of a star and the pictures of where it used to be. [NASA’s account of M31-2014-DS1](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/science-news/2026/02/12/archival-data-from-nasas-neowise-tracks-star-turning-into-black-hole/)

How does a supernova fail? In a massive star, the core eventually loses the support that has been holding up its own weight. It collapses, and a shock forms. If the shock gains enough energy to drive through the layers still falling in, it can blow much of the star into space. If it stalls and stays stalled, gravity keeps collecting material. A black hole can form while the outer layers produce a much fainter display. The failure belongs to the explosion. The collapse proceeds perfectly well. [NASA’s account of the collapse](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/science-news/2026/02/12/archival-data-from-nasas-neowise-tracks-star-turning-into-black-hole/) · [Caltech’s account of failed supernovae](https://www.caltech.edu/about/news/astronomers-find-hints-of-star-population-that-failed-to-explode)

A disappearing star gives you one address to investigate. How would you tell whether the same thing happened to stars whose light you never saw?

## What the survivors weigh

When two black holes spiral towards one another, they send out gravitational waves. The signal rises in frequency as their orbit tightens. Its shape lets astronomers estimate the masses involved, which is an extraordinary amount of information to extract from a collision between two things you cannot see.

The *Physical Review D* team analysed 153 gravitational-wave detections and estimated how common different masses are for the heavier black hole in a merging pair. The inferred distribution has a strong peak around ten times the mass of the Sun. By about fifteen solar masses, the estimated merger-rate density is roughly a hundred times lower. The distribution rises again above about sixteen. That dip is a scarcity inferred from the catalogue. You can still have individual black holes in that range. [*Physical Review D*](https://doi.org/10.1103/mnzq-w1b5)

You can’t get that distribution by counting the detections in each weight bracket. Some mergers are easier to detect than others, so a catalogue reflects both what the universe produces and what the instruments can pick up. The researchers account for that selection and for uncertainty in individual mass estimates. They find that a distinct group of black holes formed through failed supernovae could help reproduce the peak. Unusually compact stellar cores may make collapse into a black hole more likely in the relevant mass range, though the authors say core compactness alone falls short of explaining how steeply the inferred rate drops. The peak gives them a formation hypothesis to test. It gives no individual black hole a birth certificate. [*Physical Review D*](https://doi.org/10.1103/mnzq-w1b5) · [Caltech’s study summary](https://www.caltech.edu/about/news/astronomers-find-hints-of-star-population-that-failed-to-explode)

I keep thinking about the astronomer who catches AT 2016blu once. She has a brightening, a position and perhaps an evening’s worth of very good data. The next outburst might come when her telescope is pointed somewhere else. There is every chance that the most informative thing about what she saw will happen on a night she misses.

Somewhere else, a star fades and leaves a little warmth in the dust. A black hole born that way might spend millions of years in a binary before giving anyone a means of weighing it. By then the light from its birth, whatever little there was, has long since passed.

Given one clear night and a choice between the two, I’d still point the telescope at the fireworks. I’d like to think I’d remember to look at the empty patch again tomorrow.
