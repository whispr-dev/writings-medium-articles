# How Can We Possibly Be Running Out of Sand?

*The planet has deserts visible from space and beaches on every continent. Yet builders are fighting over riverbeds—and the grains beneath our feet are becoming a strategic resource.*

**By Claudia G. Petersen**

Has someone mislaid the Sahara? Have the beaches begun rationing? Are we genuinely expected to believe that a planet with dunes large enough to swallow cities is running short of the substance found inside every child’s least hygienic bucket?

And if we are running out, where has it all gone? Can we not simply drive into a desert, collect several tasteful billion tonnes and be home before the champagne warms? What possible distinction could there be between one tiny piece of rock and another?

At first glance, a sand shortage sounds like a champagne drought announced from inside the cellar. The difficulty is that the world is not running out of grains. It is running out of particular grains, in particular places, which can be removed at particular speeds without dismantling the landscapes around them.

That is a rather different crisis—and a much more interesting one.

## The Sahara objection

Let us begin with the answer everyone has already shouted at the page: deserts.

Desert sand certainly exists in magnificent quantities. It is simply not the effortless substitute it appears to be. Wind carries grains, drops them, lifts them and rubs them against one another over very long periods. Much desert sand consequently becomes extremely fine, smooth and rounded.

Concrete generally performs better with a controlled mixture of grain sizes and particles whose surfaces allow the cement paste to grip them effectively. Many wind-blown desert sands have the wrong grading, texture or shape to replace conventional construction sand without treatment, blending or a specially designed mix. Researchers are developing ways to use more of it—[including work at KAUST](https://fii.kaust.edu.sa/en/economies-of-future/sustainable-concrete-desert-sands/) that transforms local desert grains into more suitable aggregate—but “the desert is full of sand” is not yet an engineering specification.

Nor is every river or beach grain automatically perfect. Construction standards consider size distribution, strength, cleanliness, salt and mineral composition. A grain can pass the geological dress code and still be refused entry at the concrete door.

Transport matters too. Sand is heavy, bulky and, at the point of extraction, relatively cheap. Move it far enough and the journey costs more than the material. Darlings, abundance is not a delivery system. A splendid dune hundreds of kilometres from a city is not equivalent to a licensed aggregate deposit beside it, any more than a lake in Finland is an immediate solution to a thirsty restaurant in Seville.

The shortage is therefore not global in the simplest sense. It is local, regional and functional: too little of the right material near the places consuming it, or too little that can be extracted without inflicting a larger cost elsewhere.

## Sand is a size, not a pedigree

Geologists use *sand* chiefly as a size category. The US Geological Survey defines sand-sized particles as roughly [0.0625 to 2 millimetres across](https://apps.usgs.gov/thesaurus/term-simple.php?code=1011&thcode=2). They may be quartz, shell, volcanic rock or other minerals. Their composition, shape and history can differ enormously.

“Sand” is therefore less like a single ingredient and more like the phrase “something chopped small”. Chopped diamonds and chopped celery satisfy the wording while producing markedly different sandwiches.

Different industries want different sands. Glassmaking needs silica-rich material with controlled impurities. Foundries, filtration systems, ceramics, abrasives and electronics each impose their own demands. Construction consumes by far the greatest volumes, principally as aggregate in concrete, mortar, asphalt, road foundations and fill.

And a clarification before anyone at the Institution of Civil Engineers develops a twitch: cement and concrete are not the same. Cement is the binding powder; concrete combines it with water, sand and gravel. Calling cement “concrete” is like calling flour “cake”. Guests will notice the missing work.

Most of concrete’s bulk is aggregate. Every apartment block, pavement, bridge, airport, dam and motorway therefore represents an astonishing number of grains that have been persuaded to stop being scenery and become infrastructure.

## Fifty billion tonnes is not a beach holiday

The United Nations Environment Programme estimates that humanity uses about **50 billion tonnes of sand and gravel each year**, making the pair the world’s second most heavily used natural resource after water. The figure combines sand with gravel because construction consumes them together, but its scale is no less indecent for being a family account.

UNEP once translated that annual appetite into a wall around the entire planet: 27 metres high and 27 metres wide. One year’s consumption. A tasteful little border feature visible, presumably, from a great many places where it would be frowned upon.

Its [2026 global assessment](https://www.unep.org/en/news-and-stories/press-release/sand-wanted-dead-and-alive-use-it-wisely-warns-un) says sand use for buildings alone could rise by as much as 45 per cent by 2060. Cities are growing and billions of people reasonably want safe homes, roads and sanitation. This is genuine development, not an inexplicable fondness for concrete garden ornaments.

But natural sand is created and sorted by erosion, rivers, waves and time. We can empty an accessible deposit much faster than those processes can rebuild it. A resource may be geologically common and still be practically non-renewable on the schedule of a construction boom.

When nearby legal supplies diminish, extraction moves farther away, deeper underwater or into poorly regulated and illegal markets. The price paid at the building site rises. The less visible price remains at the river or coast.

## The sand was already doing something

Here is the part our normal idea of “raw material” tends to conceal: sand in nature is not waiting idly to become a tower.

In a river, it shapes the channel, filters water, provides habitat and replenishes deltas and beaches. Along a coast, it absorbs waves and separates the sea from homes, farms and freshwater aquifers. On the seabed, it supports ecosystems not improved by being mistaken for a warehouse floor.

The beach is not off duty merely because no one is sunbathing.

Remove too much sand from a riverbed and the channel can deepen. Banks become unstable. Water levels and flow patterns change. Erosion works its way upstream or downstream; bridges, farmland and homes can lose the ground that made them secure. Take sand from coasts and nearshore zones, and waves may reclaim land that no longer has its granular armour. Saltwater can intrude farther into freshwater systems.

Meanwhile, dams trap much of the sediment that rivers would naturally carry toward deltas. We interrupt the delivery upstream and mine the remaining stock downstream, a supply-chain arrangement with the austere logic of cancelling dinner and then eating the table.

The Mekong Delta provides an especially sobering example. [Recent research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13344278/) found mining there exceeded natural sediment supply by six to fifteen times, contributing to riverbed incision, erosion and salinity problems. Dams, groundwater withdrawal, subsidence and rising seas also matter, but mining adds another force to a landscape already holding itself together with less material.

The sea is increasingly part of the hunt. UNEP’s [Marine Sand Watch](https://www.unep.org/en/news-and-stories/press-release/unep-marine-sand-watch-reveals-massive-extraction-worlds-oceans) estimates that dredging removes between four and eight billion tonnes of sand and other marine sediments each year, with six billion as its central estimate. That is more than one million dump-truck loads a day. The activity can cloud water, alter nutrients, create noise, damage seabed habitats and weaken coastal resilience, depending on where and how it occurs.

This is why UNEP’s latest report makes a beautifully useful distinction. Sand placed into concrete, asphalt or glass becomes, in ecological terms, **“dead” sand**: still physically present, but removed from the natural system. Sand left in a river, delta or coast is **“alive”**, continually performing work for water security, biodiversity and protection from storms and erosion.

We have traditionally priced the grain and received the river for free.

## Are we actually going to run out?

Not in the cartoon sense. There will not be a final ceremonial grain placed into a mixer while the world’s deserts stand mysteriously empty.

What we can exhaust are accessible deposits of suitable construction sand. Extraction can become too damaging to continue, too contested for communities to accept, or too distant to remain economical. We can also lose beaches, fisheries and freshwater while insisting the commodity itself remains cheap.

“Running out” is shorthand for crossing several limits at once:

- **Geological limits:** nature does not replace useful deposits as quickly as we remove them.
- **Geographical limits:** the correct sand may be nowhere near the demand.
- **Engineering limits:** not every grain suits every use.
- **Ecological limits:** a deposit may be more valuable where it is than inside a building.
- **Governance limits:** extraction may be poorly measured, weakly regulated or illegal.

Scarcity is not always an empty shelf. Sometimes it is a full landscape from which taking more has become intolerable.

## Must every new city eat a river?

Fortunately, there is no single heroic substitute upon which the whole future must balance. There is a portfolio of less glamorous, more credible improvements.

Old concrete can become recycled aggregate. Rock can be crushed into manufactured sand. Material from demolition, excavation and some mine tailings can be recovered instead of buried. Roads can be recycled. Buildings can use material more efficiently, last longer and be adapted rather than demolished.

None of these options is magically impact-free. Crushing rock consumes energy and creates dust. Recycled material must be sorted, tested and matched to appropriate uses. Mine waste may contain contaminants. Quality standards matter because “circular” is a poor consolation if the bridge has circularly collapsed.

But the alternatives change the question from *Where can we dig next?* to *What material have we already paid to extract?* They can reduce pressure on active rivers and coasts while turning waste into supply.

We also need the stubbornly unromantic work: mapping deposits, measuring extraction, enforcing permits, protecting beaches and active nearshore systems, pricing ecological damage, and deciding that some sand is not available merely because a dredger can reach it.

## The trouble with things that look infinite

The sand crisis exposes a mistake much larger than sand.

We tend to call a resource abundant when we can see a great deal of it. But usefulness depends upon quality, location, rate of renewal and what the material is already doing. Fresh water covers very little of a watery planet. The atmosphere is immense, yet its capacity to accept pollution without consequence is not. Forests contain wood, but they are not merely timber awaiting an invoice.

Earth is not running out of tiny rocks. We are running out of convenient deposits that can be removed faster than nature restores them while leaving everything else apparently unchanged.

A desert may be full of sand and still be no answer. A riverbed may look inert and still be holding a city up. A beach may appear to be loose material and actually be a piece of infrastructure built by waves over centuries, protecting infrastructure we built last Tuesday.

Perhaps the most dangerous sentence in resource economics is: *There is plenty of it somewhere.*

The wiser question is not simply how much remains, but what the material is doing where it is—and who will discover its value only after it has gone.

Because whenever we ask, “Where will we obtain the next billion tonnes?”, another question is already waiting beneath it:

**What have we silently decided may disappear so that we can take it?**
