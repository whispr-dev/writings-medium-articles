# Why Would Anyone Need a Nearly Perfect Ball?

*It is too valuable to play with, too delicate for bare hands and so round that its imperfections must be measured in nanometres. This was all considered entirely reasonable.*

**By Claudia G. Petersen**

What sort of problem requires a $3.2 million ball that nobody is allowed to bounce? Why make it from an isotope purified beyond five nines, polish it for months, then worry about the invisible film that appeared the moment it met the atmosphere? And if perfect roundness is impossible, how close must one come before civilisation can safely weigh a bag of flour?

The object in question is about 9.4 centimetres across. It weighs approximately one kilogram and resembles a small, dark mirror that has somehow folded itself into a sphere. Its makers produced a shape so precise that, enlarged to the size of Earth, the difference between its highest and lowest points would be only a few metres.

It is not a bearing, a spacecraft component or the private desk ornament of a Bond villain with unusually restrained taste.

It is a ball made from almost pure silicon-28, and it was created to answer a question that sounds insultingly simple:

What, exactly, is a kilogram?

## The kilogram used to be a thing

For 130 years, the official answer was kept near Paris.

The International Prototype of the Kilogram—often called *Le Grand K*—was a polished cylinder of platinum-iridium made in the nineteenth century. In 1889, the world agreed that its mass was one kilogram. Not approximately one kilogram. Not one kilogram after adjustment for age, dust or a particularly humid Tuesday. It *was* the kilogram.

Even before that cylinder took the throne, the physical kilogram had acquired a taste for geopolitical farce. In 1794, French physician and botanist Joseph Dombey sailed for Philadelphia to meet Thomas Jefferson, carrying a copper metre and a one-kilogram standard then called a *grave*. A storm drove his ship into the Caribbean, where British privateers seized it. Dombey was imprisoned on Montserrat and died before he could be ransomed; the cargo was auctioned. The vessel was not sunk, incidentally. And the attackers were privateers—pirates supplied with government permission, because paperwork has always improved the manners of theft. [The standards never reached Jefferson](https://www.nist.gov/blogs/taking-measure/pirates-caribbean-metric-edition).

Did that condemn America to pounds and ounces? It is an irresistible answer and an unprovable one. Dombey’s arrival might have strengthened the case before Congress, but the United States [legalised metric measurement in 1866](https://www.nist.gov/pml/owm/metric-si/metric-policy), and in 1893 metric standards became the nation’s fundamental standards for weights and measures. The fairest verdict is that the raid may have cost America a particularly well-timed metric moment. To say it decided the next two centuries asks one unlucky voyage to carry rather more historical weight than even a kilogram should.

Copies were made and distributed to national laboratories. At intervals, some returned to France for comparison with the original. By the verification conducted from 1989 to 1991, the original group of prototypes had diverged from the master by a [median of about 25 micrograms](https://www.bipm.org/en/history-si/kilogram).

The popular version of this story says that Le Grand K was losing weight. The more accurate version is stranger: nobody could know. Perhaps the master had changed. Perhaps its copies had. Perhaps each had accumulated or surrendered slightly different films of contamination. There was no external kilogram against which to settle the matter, because the cylinder itself was the definition.

If a few atoms quietly departed Le Grand K, its mass remained exactly one kilogram by decree. The rest of the world’s kilograms would simply have become wrong.

This arrangement had served science and commerce remarkably well, but it contained an increasingly embarrassing principle. The metre had been liberated from a metal bar and tied to the speed of light. The second belonged to a transition in caesium atoms. Yet mass—the quantity underpinning medicine, engineering, trade and a great deal of anxious luggage repacking—still depended upon the continued wellbeing of one elderly object in a vault.

Metrology had a sovereign. The sovereign was not required to explain itself.

## Building the most accountable crystal ball

One route out of this predicament began with a wonderfully audacious proposal: make an object whose atoms could be counted.

Not individually, one hastens to add. Even metrologists have limits. A one-kilogram silicon-28 sphere contains roughly 2.15 × 10²⁵ atoms. Counting one every second would take many times the age of the universe, by which point the calibration certificate might be considered slightly out of date.

Instead, the International Avogadro Project used the regularity of a crystal. Silicon atoms in a single crystal occupy a repeating geometric lattice. If scientists could determine the volume occupied by one small repeating unit, know how many atoms it contained, and measure the total volume of a macroscopic crystal, they could infer the atom count without troubling each atom for identification.

Silicon was exceptionally well suited to the scheme. Decades of semiconductor manufacturing had taught humanity how to grow large, pure single crystals with very few defects. Its lattice structure was well understood. It was stable, could be polished exquisitely and—once enriched in one isotope—gave researchers atoms of almost identical mass.

The resulting spheres were [99.9995 per cent silicon-28](https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/kilogram/kilogram-silicon-spheres-and-international-avogadro-project). Natural silicon also contains silicon-29 and silicon-30; those extra neutrons give the isotopes different masses, introducing uncertainty into the average. Isotopic enrichment turned a rather mixed guest list into something much easier to audit.

Then came the shaping. Australian master optician Achim Leistner and the team at CSIRO’s Australian Centre for Precision Optics combined precision machinery with painstaking hand-finishing. A surviving project prototype in Sydney’s Powerhouse collection measures [93.6 millimetres across and one kilogram in mass](https://collection.powerhouse.com.au/object/539785). NIST estimates that the enriched project spheres cost about $3.2 million each.

Calling them “the world’s roundest objects” invites a quarrel with several extremely accomplished spacecraft gyroscopes, so let us settle for *among the roundest objects ever made*. NIST offers the more useful comparison: if one were expanded to Earth’s diameter, the vertical distance from its metaphorical deepest ocean to its tallest mountain would be [roughly three to five metres](https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/kilogram/kilogram-silicon-spheres-and-international-avogadro-project).

Earth, confronted with this standard, would require polishing.

## Why a sphere?

A sphere is not merely beautiful, although in this case it has every right to be pleased with itself.

Its volume can be calculated from a single basic quantity: diameter. In practice, researchers measure many diameters from many directions with optical interferometers, mapping every minute departure from ideal roundness and using the results to determine the mean volume. There are no corners to damage, no edges whose geometry must be separately characterised, and no privileged direction in which the object must be placed.

For a given volume, a sphere also has the smallest possible surface area. That matters because the surface is where an otherwise immaculate calculation encounters chemistry, air and the regrettable fact that laboratories are situated in reality.

The sphere’s diameter is measured to nanometre precision. X-ray interferometry determines the spacing of planes within its silicon lattice. Mass spectrometry establishes the precise isotopic composition and therefore the molar mass of the crystal. A vacuum balance measures the macroscopic mass.

The silicon crystal has a cubic repeating cell containing the equivalent of eight atoms. If *a* is the width of that cell, its volume is *a³*, and each atom can be assigned one eighth of it. Compare that microscopic volume with the sphere’s macroscopic volume and the enormous atom count emerges from geometry.

In its compact form, the calculation for the Avogadro constant is:

`N_A = 8MV / (m a³)`

Here *M* is the molar mass of the silicon, *V* the sphere’s silicon-core volume, *m* its corresponding mass and *a* the crystal lattice parameter. The method is called X-ray crystal density, or XRCD. It is “counting atoms” in much the same way that one counts theatre seats by knowing the number of rows and seats per row—provided the theatre contains about twenty-one septillion places and the architect works in fractions of a nanometre.

## The intolerable weight of almost nothing

At everyday precision, the polished sphere looks featureless. At parts-per-billion precision, it becomes crowded with complications.

Silicon exposed to air forms a nanometre-scale layer of silicon oxide. Water and carbon-containing molecules can adhere to that layer. Inside the crystal there may be vacancies where an atom ought to be, or trace impurities where an atom of something else has taken the seat. The act of polishing can leave minute residues. Each effect alters either the mass, the apparent optical diameter or both.

Consequently, researchers had to characterise the surface chemically, subtract the mass and volume of its outer layers, and correct for imperfections within the lattice. The [technical accounting for an XRCD sphere](https://www.bipm.org/documents/20126/28432586/working-document-ID-11317/27f11c71-e897-1af4-5c72-aef498cffe74) explicitly includes surface-layer mass, impurities and vacancies.

At this level, a fingerprint would not be a smudge. It would be an unauthorised mass transfer.

This is why near-perfect roundness was not a piece of scientific jewellery. Any uncertainty in diameter is magnified when diameter becomes volume; any ambiguity at the surface contaminates the distinction between the silicon being counted and the material merely travelling with it. The researchers did not require philosophical perfection. They required every imperfection to be measurable.

That distinction is the whole craft of metrology. Precision does not mean possessing an object without flaws. It means knowing the flaws well enough that they have nowhere left to hide.

## The perfect object built to end perfect objects

The silicon spheres did not win the right to become a new Le Grand K. That would merely have exchanged a cylinder-shaped monarch for a spherical one.

Instead, they worked alongside another extraordinary instrument, the Kibble balance. The balance connected mass to electrical measurements and the Planck constant; the silicon spheres connected macroscopic mass to atomic spacing and the Avogadro constant. Because the Planck and Avogadro constants are linked through other exceptionally well-known physical quantities, the two approaches could test one another. [Measurements from both contributed](https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/kilogram/kilogram-silicon-spheres-and-international-avogadro-project) to the values used for the revised system.

On 20 May 2019, the old artefact definition ceased to rule. The kilogram is now defined by fixing the Planck constant at exactly [6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds](https://www.bipm.org/en/si-base-units/kilogram). The [Avogadro constant was fixed exactly too](https://www.bipm.org/en/si-base-units/mole), at 6.02214076 × 10²³ entities per mole.

The direction of the silicon calculation could now be reversed. Before 2019, a sphere whose mass was traceable to Le Grand K helped scientists determine the constants. Afterwards, the fixed constants and the measured crystal could be used to realise a mass. The BIPM notes that, in principle, a national metrology institute can now realise the kilogram using a Kibble or joule balance, or by applying [the XRCD technique](https://www.bipm.org/en/-/2021-kg-consensus).

“In principle” is doing some elegant heavy lifting there. A multimillion-dollar isotopically enriched sphere, X-ray interferometry and surface chemistry at parts per billion do not constitute a kitchen-scale recipe. But the principle matters: the kilogram no longer resides in one guarded body. A sufficiently capable laboratory can construct a route back to it from properties of nature available everywhere.

There is something almost extravagant about the achievement. Scientists assembled an international collaboration, purified an isotope, grew an astonishing crystal and commissioned one of the finest spheres ever made—all to ensure that no single material object would ever again be indispensable.

The old kilogram derived its authority from custody: this is the object; we have preserved it; therefore, trust it. The new kilogram derives its authority from reproducibility: these are the laws and constants; perform the experiment; arrive here yourself.

We often confuse permanence with successful safekeeping. Lock a thing behind enough doors and it begins to look eternal. Yet the deeper form of permanence may be a recipe—knowledge that survives the loss of any one vault, institution, nation or exquisitely polished possession.

So why would anyone need a nearly perfect ball?

To make certain that, eventually, nobody did.
