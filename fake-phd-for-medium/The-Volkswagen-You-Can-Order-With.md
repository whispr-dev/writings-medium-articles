# The Volkswagen You Can Order With Chips

*It has an official part number. Removing it from one corner of the company caused a national argument.*

**By Claudia G. Petersen**

Why would a former German chancellor take an interest in Volkswagen part number 199 398 500 A? What could make that particular item worth defending, and how did a decision about where to offer it become a public argument?

The code has the reassuring dullness of industrial bookkeeping. You can almost imagine it beside an exploded diagram, with a tiny arrow pointing towards something expensive and difficult to reach. Knowing Volkswagen makes it hardly narrows the possibilities. A car contains an impressive number of things you would struggle to identify on a table.

This one, however, is remarkably easy to recognise. Its association with the company goes back more than half a century, and understanding its appeal requires a small adjustment to the way we picture a car factory.

Think about the people working there. Eventually, they need lunch.

The part is a sausage.

Specifically, Volkswagen's currywurst: a spiced sausage commonly served sliced, covered in curry ketchup, and accompanied by chips. The company began making its version in 1973, and production takes place in the butchery of its Service Factory at Wolfsburg. It carries the designation *Volkswagen Originalteil*: Volkswagen original part. [The product's history and part number are documented here](https://www.tageblatt.de/Nachrichten/VW-erreicht-Absatzrekord-bei-der-Currywurst-644619.html).

There is something particularly pleasing about that last detail. A branded sausage would be amusing enough. Giving it the administrative dignity of a replacement component suggests that somebody understood exactly how funny the whole arrangement was.

Yet the reason for its existence is entirely practical. A factory is also a place where people spend a substantial portion of their lives. Feeding them belongs to its daily operation just as surely as moving materials through the gates. Volkswagen's chefs developed the recipe, which the company says remains known to only a few people. [Volkswagen's own account](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/a-taste-of-germany-arrives-in-the-us-with-vw-gewurz-ketchup-brand-302254619.html).

Seen from the canteen, making sausages becomes considerably less surprising. What is surprising is how far the sausages travelled beyond it.

In 2024, Volkswagen sold approximately 8.55 million across its sausage range, according to figures published by IG Metall at Volkswagen. That total includes the classic currywurst, a hot-dog version and a smaller beef variant. Around nine out of ten of the 6.3 million classic sausages were sold outside the company. An employee meal had developed a substantial life of its own. [IG Metall's sales breakdown](https://www.igm-bei-vw.de/meldung/855-millionen-stueck-currywurst-absatz-erzielt-auch-2024-neuen-rekord).

For perspective, the Volkswagen passenger-car brand delivered approximately 4.8 million vehicles that year. The entire Volkswagen Group, including its other marques and commercial vehicles, delivered about nine million. The distinction matters when you encounter the irresistible claim that Volkswagen sells more sausages than cars: the comparison works for the passenger-car brand, while the wider group remains ahead. [Volkswagen brand figures](https://www.volkswagen-newsroom.com/en/press-releases/volkswagen-delivers-around-48-million-vehicles-worldwide-in-2024-18950), [Volkswagen Group figures](https://www.volkswagen-group.com/en/press-releases/volkswagen-group-with-9-million-deliveries-after-strong-fourth-quarter-18965).

Naturally, counting individual sausages and cars tells us very little about their relative importance to the accounts. Lunch invites more repeat purchases than a hatchback. But millions of purchases do establish something useful: this curiosity has customers willing to return for another helping.

The ketchup acquired enthusiasts of its own. In September 2024, Volkswagen of America announced a limited giveaway of its GewÃ¼rz Ketchup through its merchandise shop. The announcement featured enthusiast Jamie Orr, who had been bringing bottles back from Germany as souvenirs and giving them away at gatherings. Even the American ketchup promotion came with a part number. [Volkswagen's announcement](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/a-taste-of-germany-arrives-in-the-us-with-vw-gewurz-ketchup-brand-302254619.html).

Consider the journey involved. A sauce served with a factory meal becomes something a visitor carries home, then something a car enthusiast gives to another enthusiast. Eventually, the company recognises a promotional opportunity in a habit its customers have already formed.

A bottle of ketchup makes an unusually good souvenir in that situation. You can share it. You can explain why you brought it. The explanation is part of the gift, and considerably more entertaining than an account of where you bought a keyring.

Inside the factory, though, a familiar lunch can carry a different kind of significance. That became apparent in 2021, when one Wolfsburg canteen moved to a menu without meat and the currywurst became the subject of a much larger dispute.

Former chancellor Gerhard SchrÃ¶der joined the criticism, invoking the sausage's place in the working lives of skilled production employees. Suddenly, a catering decision had acquired a national political audience. Here was a former head of government defending something normally served with a small fork. [Reporting on the dispute](https://www.euronews.com/culture/2023/09/05/currywurst-the-german-street-food-favourite-returns-to-volkswagens-central-canteen).

The scale of the reaction becomes more striking once you learn the scale of the change. It affected one canteen. More than thirty other canteens and kiosks at the plant continued offering the sausage. It returned to the affected canteen in 2023. There had been plenty of opportunities to obtain lunch throughout the supposed crisis. [ARD's account of what changed](https://www.tagesschau.de/wirtschaft/unternehmen/volkswagen-currywurst-rekord-100.html).

It would be easy to laugh and leave the story there. There is certainly enough material. But the gap between a modest change and an enormous reaction deserves a little attention.

An outsider sees an item on a menu. Someone who has eaten it during years of working days may associate it with particular colleagues, familiar conversations, or the relief of sitting down halfway through a difficult shift. Those associations will differ from person to person. None has to be especially grand to make an apparently minor decision feel personal.

This is my reading of the fuss: people can experience the removal of a familiar thing as a decision about how much their preferences count. Once that happens, explaining how easily they could get the same thing elsewhere may miss the objection entirely.

That does not settle the argument in favour of the sausage. Employees who want different food belong to the workplace too. Their preferences deserve room, and a tradition cannot acquire a permanent veto simply by becoming old. A company has to manage changing tastes alongside practical responsibilities. The interesting question is how the people affected get heard.

There is a temptation, when telling this story, to turn Volkswagen into a cosy employer with a charmingly eccentric kitchen. A canteen tradition offers only a small window into a much larger institution. It cannot tell us everything about how that institution treats people.

In December 2024, Volkswagen and its unions reached an agreement envisaging more than 35,000 fewer jobs at VW AG's German operations by 2030, alongside a new employment-protection scheme. These were negotiated changes, with consequences extending far beyond a menu. [Reuters on the agreement](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/main-points-volkswagens-deal-with-unions-german-sites-jobs-2024-12-20/).

A business needs customers and a workable balance sheet. Preserving every existing arrangement indefinitely is a promise no employer can responsibly make. Even so, there is a tension worth noticing when a company benefits from people's affection while having to make decisions that may weaken their attachment to it.

The employee who takes pride in a workplace contributes something beyond the hours on a contract. They may recommend it, defend it, or pass on knowledge they could have kept to themselves. A place that inspires that kind of commitment gains something valuable. Its value becomes awkward to calculate precisely when a familiar service, job or routine appears on a list of possible savings.

The currywurst makes this visible because it is so gloriously ordinary. A company can give lunch an official number; the people eating it supply meanings that no inventory system can record. Some of those meanings eventually become useful enough to market overseas in a bottle of ketchup.

We readily understand why a business would want that affection. The more difficult question concerns the obligation that comes with accepting it.

If a company is entitled to ask people for their loyalty, what are those people entitled to ask for in return?
